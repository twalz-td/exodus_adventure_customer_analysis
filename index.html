1

Include all three customer types

Repeaters, one-time customers, and first-booking customers all belong in the seed. "Never Travelled" in this file means a customer who hadn't taken a prior Exodus trip at the time of booking — they are actual paying customers. Critically, first-booking customers have the highest average revenue per pax (£2,500) in the entire file. Excluding them would have taught the model to deprioritize exactly the kind of high-value new customer Exodus needs to acquire.

2

Score on composite value, not revenue alone

Revenue per pax alone favors one-time premium bookers who may never return. The composite (50% revenue, 30% trip count, 20% booking window) rewards customers who are valuable AND loyal. This teaches the model to find committed Exodus customers, not just high-price one-shots.

3

Prioritize the 14 highest-fit Mosaic clusters

A01–A05, B06–B08, D13–D14, K39–K40, L41–L42 account for the majority of the file's revenue and show the strongest alignment with Exodus's brand values — affluence, cultural engagement, environmental awareness, active lifestyle. Customers outside these clusters who score well on composite value are retained; the Mosaic filter ensures the seed's demographic core is defensible.

4

Trim the bottom 35% on composite value

Removing the low-engagement, low-spend tail reduces noise that would pull k-means centroids away from the high-value profile. The 35% trim is conservative — retaining 13,932 of 33,504 total records, with sufficient volume across all three customer types for robust segmentation. The bottom tier being removed consists primarily of low-spend, short booking-window records with no Mosaic alignment — customers unlikely to represent the Exodus brand's addressable growth opportunity.
